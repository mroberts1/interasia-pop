**Summary**

Oyama's paper examines creative labor in Japanese media industries, arguing that the Western "creative class" rhetoric of autonomy-plus-precarity doesn't apply. In Japan, creative managers (editors, producers, studio executives) enjoy lifetime employment and hierarchical security, while primary creators (mangaka, musicians, actors) face acute precarity. The decoupling of creative managerial labor from creative risk produces different kinds of output than the Western freelance model — demonstrated through manga editorial practice at Shueisha.

**Key points**

- Japanese creative industries operate on membership-type employment, not Western freelance
- Creative managers (editors, A&R, producers) have lifetime security; creators below them face precarity
- Shukatsu (formalized graduate recruitment) funnels elite university graduates into these managerial roles
- 200:1 competition ratios at top publishers (Shueisha, Kodansha, Shogakukan)
- Uncreativity (Bilton's term): methodical editorial guidance and stability as counterbalance to creator innovation
- Editor-mangaka relationship combines long-term mentorship with structural power asymmetry
- This labor structure helps explain the durability and global reach of Japanese media franchises

ABSTRACT

ARTICLE HISTORY

The Weekly Shōnen Jump, published by Shueisha, is widely seen as the
most important publication in global manga culture and home to some of
the world’s most iconic media franchises, including Dragon Ball, and yet
remains largely understudied. This article interrogates how Shōnen
Jump’s success, steered by editors who are often deemed ‘uncreative’
within Japan’s inflexible employment framework, challenges the Western
ideal of creativity that is associated with autonomy and flexibility. The
inquiry is conducted through two primary processes: firstly, the recruit­
ment of editors – considered as creative managers – through ‘Shukatsu,’ a
Japanese system that predominantly favors elite university graduates,
who often lack a passion for manga or relevant education and work
experience; and secondly, the dynamics of their collaborative relation­
ships with manga artists. Highlighting Shueisha’s exceptional job security
and stability, akin to a ‘last paradise’ for a select few creative professionals,
this examination challenges the homogenized Western narrative of crea­
tive labor, as a class characterized by precarity and challenges the con­
ventional perception of creativity itself.

Introduction: powerful creative industries and weak interest in creativity
As the world’s third-largest economy, Japan has built a formidable reputation as a major force in
global cultural production. Japanese video games, anime, manga, fashion, films, architecture, litera­
ture, contemporary arts, and design have left a distinctive mark on global popular culture. In the
2021 ‘The 25 Highest-Grossing Media Franchises of All Time’ (Hallman 2021), Japan’s Pokémon
topped the rankings at $92 billion, followed by Hello Kitty at $80 billion, comfortably ahead of
Winnie the Pooh ($75 billion), Micky Mouse ($70 billion), and Star Wars ($65 billion). In all, media
franchises originating in Japan accounted for 10 of the top 25, second only to the US with 13. Of
these Dragon Ball ($24 billion), Fist of the North Star ($21 billion), One Piece ($21 billion), and Yu-GiOh ($19 billion) all originated as manga series in Weekly Shōnen Jump (WJ hereafter),1 published by
Shueisha Inc., one of the most important, yet least studied, media companies in the world.
Despite the global success of Japanese popular culture, there’s a muted interest in the
concepts of creativity or creative industries in Japan. For instance, the Japanese term for
‘creative industries,’ sōzō sangyō, is seldom employed beyond a select group of bureaucrats
and scholars (Oyama 2015). This poses a contradiction: while Japan’s creative sectors enjoy
global prominence, many insiders and academics seem indifferent to the wider discourse on
creativity. No company exemplifies this paradox more vividly than Shueisha, which produces

Literature review: creative managers, recruitment, and uncreativity
In this article, I use the term creative workers to refer to individuals involved in the production of
symbolic goods in the creative and cultural industries (Banks 2007). Arguing against the utopian and
romantic celebration of creative labour as autonomous, self-expressive, and fulfilling (e.g. Florida
2002; Howkins 2007; Landry 2000), creative labor research stresses that work in cultural and creative
industries is characterized by high levels of insecurity, casualization, and reproduction of alreadyexisting inequalities (Banks, Gill, and Taylor 2013; Brook, O’Brien, and Taylor 2020; Curtin and Sanson
2016; Hesmondhalgh and Baker 2011). Following the pioneering works of Ross (2009) and McRobbie
(2002, 2016), this body of research shows how mostly young creative workers willingly subject
themselves to seeming exploitation and precarity (Hesmondhalgh and Baker 2011, 6–7).
To unravel this paradox, substantial studies have explored how motivations and work ethics in
creative fields are driven by factors like autonomy (Banks 2010), a passion for creativity (Kim 2013), or
self-expression (Duffy 2017). These studies paint and critique an idealized image of a creative worker,
where the pursuit of creativity is closely tied to flexibility, freedom, and even enduring ‘precarity’ in
their professional lives. In this context, while aiming to critique the inequality and injustices faced by
many creative workers, critical studies of creative labor often present a homogenous portrayal of
creative work as inherently intertwined with precarity. This article offers a case study from Shueisha,
where creativity is decoupled from precarity, with all its faults and a set of distinct problems, and
thereby presents a more nuanced understanding of creative labor in a global landscape.
This section serves to: 1) Conceptualize Shueisha editors as creative managers; 2) Explore the
recruitment contexts within Japanese media industries, along with a broader review of trends in
recruitment literature in creative labour studies; 3) Examine key literature regarding the collaboration
between creative managers and primary creative workers; 4) examines key literature in creative labor
studies in Japan and East Asia.

Manga editors as creative managers
Critical creative labour studies recognize that there is a division of labor in creative industries.
Hesmondhalgh and Baker (2011, 9–10) group creative workers into five categories: primary creative
workers, such as manga authors; creative managers, such as magazine editors; administrators;
executives; and unskilled labor. The contributions, prestige, security, and financial rewards differ
among these groups. Creative managers often act as intermediaries between profit-focused owners
and executives, and creative personnel, seeking success and reputation through innovative works
(Hesmondhalgh 2019, 92).
Moreover, although they all form part of the creative production process, some groups, particu­
larly primary creative workers, have received more scholarly attention than others, such as creative
managers, which, as a group, have received relatively scarce attention.2 Yet, existing research (e.g.
Havens 2014; Lotz 2014) underscores the significant influence of creative managers in cultural
production. Havens observes that their presumptions about what is commercially viable, referred
to as ‘industry lore’ significantly shape the range of texts that are perceived as likely to succeed
(Havens 2014, 31).
In Western literature on creative labor, distinction between creative managers and primary
creative workers in terms of job security and worsening working conditions are often overlooked
(e.g. Boston and Duffy 2016). Both are frequently seen as equally vulnerable to precarity –
a perspective in sharp contrast to the practices at Shueisha. In this article, I will frame manga editors
as creative managers who belong to a higher and more secure layer in the production of manga
compared to mangaka, who are primary creative workers and face the same precarious conditions
similar to those depicted in much of the Western literature. Viewed in this light, further qualification
is needed. This ‘paradise’ is carefully constructed and maintained for creative managers, to the
exclusion of mangaka, who are primary creative workers. Understanding this hierarchy is crucial, and
an examination of the recruitment system that influences the interplay between these two cate­
gories of workers, editors and mangaka, will be pivotal.

Recruitment dynamics in the Japanese masukomi industry
The Masukomi industry, Japan’s cornerstone in cultural and creative industries like broadcasting,
publishing, newspapers, and advertising, has exhibited remarkable stability and insularity. This
resilience has led to a sustained dominance by established entities such as Kodansha (established
in 1909), Shogakukan (established in 1922), and Shueisha (established in 1926), fending off competi­
tion from the Western companies (Oyama 2015). These companies’ recruitment practices, deeply
rooted in a ‘membership-type’ employment system where companies treat employees as lifelong
members of the company, provide lifetime employment, seniority-based promotions, age-based
salary and bonuses, and a suite of benefits that often exceed those of large banks and manufacturing
giants like Toyota and Sony (Seike 2022). Due to globalization, some Japanese companies are shifting
toward ‘job-type employment’ where focus is on the specific role or job at hand, emphasizing skills
and individual performance with less emphasis on job security. Nevertheless, this transition is slow
and has ‘not replaced but been layered upon the existing practices’ (Kambayashi 2015; Rear 2022,
509). Many influential conservative politicians have placed their offspring in these companies
through nepotism, which further underscores the stability and conservatism inherent in these
sectors.3 To gain membership into this elite class of creative managers involves navigating the
formal and organized Shukatsu process – a nationwide recruitment method unlike any other
globally, which will be explain in detail later.
In contrast, Western creative industries often embrace a more fluid, ‘job-type’ employment model.
Recruitment therein typically targets only professionals with relevant experience, who are also more
likely to have relevant education compared to their Japanese counterparts. However, there is
a dearth of literature on the recruitment of creative managers specifically. Existing studies highlight

companies like Shueisha in Japan. This gap in the literature justifies a focused case study on how
the Japanese recruitment model, specifically for creative managers, compares with their Western
counterparts. The upcoming case study aims to empirically explore Shueisha’s distinct recruitment
and employment practices, thus contributing to a broader understanding of the dynamics at play in
the recruitment of creative managers within the unique Japanese context.

Uncreativity: collaboration between editors and mangaka
The second part of the case study examines the collaboration between creative managers and
primary creative workers. The concept of ‘uncreativity’ proposed by Chris Bilton (2015) highlights
a crucial counterbalance in the creative process, challenging the preeminence of novelty by empha­
sizing the refinement and valuation of existing ideas. He articulates ‘uncreativity’ as a force that
provides stability, asserting it ‘provid[es] a necessary ballast’ against the relentless quest for innova­
tion (2). In manga production, editors manifest ‘uncreativity’ by guiding creative flows towards
‘closure and resolution’ (12), ensuring that works are not only novel but also possess enduring value.
Bilton underscores that ‘uncreativity’ involves a methodical approach to development, characterized
by self-doubt and criticism, serving as a check against ‘change for change’s sake’ (11). This philosophy is
further explored in the context of manga production at WJ, where editors critically assess and integrate
new ideas within the publication’s long and successful tradition. ‘uncreativity’ is thus positioned not as
an attack on creativity, but as a necessary critique of ‘creativity as “mere novelty”’ (12), with skepticism
and resistance to change being vital to the creative process of manga production.
The symbiotic relationship between manga editors and writers might showcase ‘uncreativity’ as
a pivotal element that infuses creativity with depth, ensuring that manga not only captivates but also
resonates with cultural expectations of readers aided by various mechanisms such as Readers’ Polls.
Bilton’s insights into ‘uncreativity’ reveal the indispensable role of editors in the manga industry,
whose evaluative input ensures that new series are more than just inventive – they are meaningful
contributions to WJ and its long tradition of storytelling and visual aesthetics.
Transitioning from theory to practice, the subsequent case study on Shueisha aims to not just
examine but also enrich Bilton’s concept by revealing a complex layer in the relationship between
the creative and the ‘uncreative’. Unlike the dynamics seen in flatter organizational structures, such
as those between advertising managers and creators (Bérubé and Demers 2019), the WJ case study
will uncover the more nuanced and hierarchical interplay at Shueisha. Here, the distinction between
creative managers and artists is not merely functional; it underscores the disparities in social status
and job security that characterize the manga industry’s intricate hierarchy.

Creative labor studies in East Asia
Finally, there exist studies on creative workers in China (Alacovska and Gill 2019; de Kloet, Lin, and
Chow 2020; Lin 2019), South Korea (Kim 2019; Kim and Lee 2022), and Hong Kong (Tse 2022), but
research on creative workers in Japan remains scarce. There have been only a limited number of
studies examining the working environments of animators in Japan (Condry 2013; Matsunaga 2017,
2020; Mōri 2011); these works primarily apply the sociology of work perspective to discuss the work
ethics governing freelance animators. None of these studies, however, situate their research within
the contemporary discourse on creative labor. In her ethnographic research on Kodansha, a major
publisher, a British Japanese studies scholar Sharon Kinsella (2000, 162–201) touches upon power
relations between editors and manga authors, highlighting the gap in education levels between the
two as a source of stratification. However, Kinsella’s research is primarily situated within the field of
Japanese studies and, despite its empirical insights, doesn’t align closely with this project’s focus on
creative labor studies.
Scholars who are working on the topics of creative labor in Asia do not simply appropriate the
theoretical concepts from the Western cases; rather, they attempt to carefully contextualize them,
raising the question of how differences found among different contexts might then speak to
theoretical debates on precarity in creative labor. This article aims to contribute to this body of
literature by providing an original contribution to the study of Japan’s prominent but underresearched media companies as well as, as I have argued earlier, insights into the studies of creative
managers and recruitment within the broader creative industries.

Shueisha, the Weekly Shonen Jump, and the recruitment
Company profile
Shueisha is a comprehensive publishing company founded in 1926 that publishes a wide range of
magazines, manga, picture books, light novels, educational books, reference books, novels, and
other books. It had over 700 employees and recorded revenues of over 200 billion yen (US$1.8 billion)
in 2021, making it one of the three largest publishers in Japan and around fifteenth largest in the
world, similar in size to Condé Nast. In 2018, Shueisha, Kodansha, and Shogakukan together had
2,400 employees and generated 471 billion yen in revenue, accounting for 4.5% of the publishing
workforce but 37% of the publishing market (METI 2018).
The WJ was launched in 1968 to compete with Kodansha’s Weekly Shōnen Magazine and
Shogakukan’s Weekly Shōnen Sunday, soon surpassing its rivals to become the best-selling manga
magazine of all time. The manga series that run in the magazine targets young male readers and
contain many action scenes and a fair amount of ‘gags’. Famously, friendship, effort, and victory
represent WJ’s guiding principles; these themes are found in one form or another in nearly all WJ
manga. In 1994, the magazine’s circulation reached 6.53 million copies a week, a world record for
a manga publication. Circulation began to decline in the early 2000s and now hovers at around
1.8 million, although the growth of digital distribution gave Shueisha its best-ever financial year in
2021. Successful WJ manga series have been turned into anime, video games, toys, novels, liveaction films, and theme parks with several of them turning to global media franchises.
The data sources used in this case study include Shueisha recruitment websites for the years
2018–2022; several recruitment guidebooks for university students; newspaper and magazine
articles; published autobiographies and interviews of current and former Shueisha WJ editors;
television documentaries about WJ; the LinkedIn profiles of current and former Shueisha employees;
and interviews I conducted with former and active magazine editors at Shueisha. For additional
background, I also interviewed two dozen university students in my seminar who are involved in job
hunting in media industries, including at Shueisha. I collected tweets and other social media
communications by job-hunting students from recruitment-related websites.

The recruitment process at Shueisha
It’s a common and almost ideologically driven practice for creative workers to move, or at least
aspire to move, from one employer to another in search of better opportunities and lifestyles
(Chow 2017; Florida 2005). In stark contrast, Shueisha primarily recruits inexperienced university
graduates who remain with the company until retirement. Each year, Shueisha launches
a website tailored for students participating in shūshoku katsudō (job hunting activity), colloqui­
ally known as shūkatsu. Shūkatsu represents the coordinated recruitment of new university
graduates at a national level, a process that is significantly more structured and formal than
job recruitment methods seen elsewhere globally. This website offers valuable insights not only
into working conditions, recruitment, and promotion policies but also into the qualities and selfperceptions deemed crucial and attractive in both successful applicants and Shueisha employees
(Shueisha 2022a).
There are two paths to secure a ‘regular’ position at Shueisha: one is through regular recruitment
via the shūkatsu process, and the other is mid-career recruitment for candidates outside the shūkatsu
system. However, between 2018 and 2023, Shueisha didn’t openly advertise mid-career roles. While
they do hire mid-career professionals for specialized roles like rights management, these employees
typically have fewer opportunities than ‘regular’ university hires, who are primarily recruited through
shūkatsu and who form the core cadre of the company hierarchy at major Japanese firms like
Shueisha. As a result, shūkatsu-based regular recruitment is essentially the primary, and possibly the
exclusive, entry point to Shueisha. This approach sharply contrasts with Western hiring practices. For
instance, major Western publishers like Conde Nast (2022) sort job listings by location, category, and
level, typically seeking candidates with extensive, specific experience. Even intern applicants are
usually pursuing a related degree.
In 2023, Shueisha reported a total of 764 employees, of which some were on limited-term
contracts. Over the preceding five years, the company consistently recruited approximately 20
new employees annually, exclusively through regular recruitment, maintaining a balanced gender
distribution. The selection process is fiercely competitive, with an estimated 200 applicants for each
available position (Motoki 2017). According to the specifications on Shueisha’s (2022a) recruitment
portal, eligible applicants should be born post-2 April 1997, and be prospective graduates by
March 2023 from a four-year academic institution, either in Japan or abroad. This stipulation implies
that successful candidates, regardless of their academic major, will be 25 years or younger at the
commencement of their employment, thereby potentially excluding individuals who have pursued
prolonged overseas studies or possess relevant advanced degrees. Historically, Shueisha’s recruit­
ment has been heavily skewed towards elite academic institutions, predominantly the University of
Tokyo and Waseda University. Only once in recent years has it hired a student from one of Japan’s
roughly two dozen non-elite universities, mostly art schools, that offer publishing or manga-related
courses of study.4
Shueisha offers some of the highest salaries in Japan. Young employees, those in their 20s, can
earn up to 10 million yen annually (approximately $93,000 using a 10-year average exchange rate) –
a benchmark beyond the reach of 95% of Japanese workers. The company provides an array of
benefits, including paid maternity and paternity leaves, company-sponsored mortgages, and allow­
ances for child education, among others. Unlike many other major Japanese corporations with
multiple offices domestically and internationally, Shueisha’s sole location is in Tokyo. This means
employees don’t face potential unwanted transfers. The combination of competitive pay, benefits,
and job stability makes Shueisha a highly sought-after employer.
Another notable feature of Shueisha’s hiring process is its approach to recruitment, termed
‘ikkatsu saiyō’ or non-job-specific hiring. The company’s official website emphasizes that they do
not recruit for designated roles like editing or sales. Instead, after a multifaceted training period –
which includes lectures, on-site training across various departments, bookstore visits, excursions to
printing companies, and team-building activities – new hires are placed in departments that match
their demonstrated skills during a short training (Tsukuru 2021a, 2021b). This is common practice at

The interview process at Shueisha
In Shueisha’s (2020) shūkatsu process, aimed at students graduating in March 2020, the application
journey started in March 2019 with entry sheets, followed by an aptitude test, a first interview,
a second interview coupled with a written exam, and three additional interviews, involving a range
of interviewers from junior editors to executives (Tsukuru Publishing 2021a, 128–9).
The entry sheet required candidates to indicate their preferred department (typically editing or
advertising), outline their ideal job, propose a business plan, and recount personal stories, including
significant interpersonal relationships, life-changing events, and their responses to challenges. They
were also queried on their favorite Shueisha products across various genres, such as magazines,
books, comics, film, theater, television, live performances, and exhibitions.
By mid-March, selected students engaged in an initial online interview. Those who advanced
underwent a second, in-person interview and a written exam, which spanned topics from American
presidents and Japanese comedians to short essays on Dostoevsky, pop culture queries, and aspects
of Japanese traditional arts, complex idiomatic expressions. The second interview was held in
a spacious meeting room, where candidates were urged to forego the traditional, dark shūkatsu
suits in favor of attire that would more accurately reflect their individuality. In this round, they
articulated their publishing interests, brainstormed business initiatives for their chosen departments,
critiqued Shueisha publications, and shared insights into their reading and viewing habits. They were
also probed on how they would adapt if placed in a department, not of their choosing (Tsukuru
Publishing 2021a, 112–3).
Applicants have stated that while rigorous, the preparatory process is manageable, informed by
guidance from alumni, specialized shūkatsu prep schools, and specialized shūkatsu media. Notably,
a candidate’s ability to present engaging narratives, demonstrate quick thinking, and possess
a broad cultural knowledge often outweighs the importance of formal education or extensive
professional experience in the selection process.

Introducing first-year employees
Every year, Shueisha introduces its new recruits on its recruitment website in friendly and entertain­
ing ways. In 2020, Ms. A, assigned to Non-no, a women’s fashion magazine, held a degree in political
economy but was passionate about good comedies and good-looking men (Shueisha 2019). She
grew up reading manga, which unsurprisingly left her with limited fashion knowledge. She didn’t
dislike fashion but questioned her fit as a fashion magazine editor. Her introduction included
a remarkable transformation, from a conventional candidate in a suit to a vibrant advocate for Nonno, sporting a multicolored dotted shirt, denim, and a pair of sneakers. The 2023 recruitment website
introduced Mr. B, an intercollegiate soccer player who nearly turned pro (Shueisha 2022b). His dream
job would have been the sports sports magazine Sportiva, but he was assigned to WJ. His boss
praised his tireless work ethic and willingness to put in long hours, qualities often found in
individuals with athletic backgrounds and valued by the masukomi industry befit for creative
manager class.
A review of Shueisha’s recruitment websites from 2018 to 2022 revealed a recurring theme – new
employees frequently found themselves in roles divergent from their initial passions, interests, or
formal education. These ‘mismatches’ were typical among freshly minted Japanese university
graduates. Shueisha portrayed them as amusing episodes and real-life lessons, emphasizing that
the ‘passion for work’ that might draw creative workers elsewhere was not always taken into

Significantly, the Shueisha website disclosed the results of a questionnaire survey of students who

showed that only the three largest publishers in Japan could compete with the salary and benefits
offered by major companies in other sectors. Notably, only 56.5% considered the publishing sector
as their top choice before commencing their job search, and as many as 30% ranked it lower than
their fourth choice. This means in theory, as many as 44% would have gone to Sony, Mitsui, or Dentsu
rather than Shueisha had they received an offer. It was clear that for most surveyed, their interest in
publishing was contingent on receiving a top-tier salary, and they seem to have no intention of
settling for less to work within the publishing industry. In fact, the most well-known current editor of
the online edition of WJ called Jump+, Lin Shihei, openly admitted to receiving multiple job offers
from companies in manufacturing and insurance but chose Shueisha for its higher salary.
Shueisha’s commitment to conventional recruitment presents a paradox in the creative indus­
tries. Despite global trends favoring self-enterprising and entrepreneurial spirits, Shueisha continues
to try to secure Japan’s top graduates with the promise of stability and competitive benefits,
a strategy that defies the rising call for a more Western-style employment system even by the
Japanese government as part of its drive to new capitalism (Kantei 2023). In this context, Shueisha’s
system, distinctively decoupling creativity from the precarity and emphasizing stability and loyalty, is
designed to nurture and systematically develop creative talents over time. Contrasting with the fluid
and individualistic nature of Western creative industries, this approach fosters a consistent cultiva­
tion of creativity, rooted in long-term professional growth and internal mentorship. This not only
reflects cultural and systemic differences but also sets the stage for understanding the distinct
collaborative dynamics between editors and artists, a key focus of the following section.”

The WJ editors and their collaborative relationship with mangaka
This section examines the career progression and working conditions of WJ editors before exploring
the intricate collaborative dynamics between these editors and freelance mangaka. This will shed
light on how traditional business models intersect with the fluid, often unpredictable nature of
creative industries.

The making of a WJ editor
Those lucky students who are hired by Shueisha and then assigned to WJ, become a part of a WJ
editing team. The team consists of five or so groups, each comprising five to six editors. An individual
editor manages between one to seven mangaka with a serialization. At the helm of the team is the
editor-in-chief, whose decisions, influenced by industry lore (Havens 2014) play a pivotal role in
determining magazine content (see also Boston and Duffy 2016, 215). Such power confers on WJ
editors-in-chief a mystic aura, which subjects them to intense media attention and has often made
them the subject of other popular culture.5
WJ‘s editor-in-chief profiles highlight a contrast between creative and uncreative aspects of
production. Historically, all 11 WJ editors-in-chief have been Japanese males recruited directly by
Shueisha. Unlike renowned Vogue editor-in-chief Anna Wintour, who accumulated experience
across various publishers, Shueisha promotes its editors-in-chief internally. They adhere to
a ‘purebred’ policy (Nishimura 1994, 321), in which WJ, while being subjected to Shueisha’s job
rotation policy, primarily recruits new students each spring who are not yet indoctrinated in other

938

S. OYAMA

divisions. Initially, WJ wasn’t a top choice within Shueisha. Now, with its hit series and global impact,
it has become a coveted position. Editors with successful series often stay longer, sometimes as long
as 15 years, bypassing the usual rotation, highlighting their key role in WJ‘s success (1101.com 2023).
Interestingly, there’s a noticeable lack of diversity among WJ’s editors-in-chief in terms of gender,
background, and even passion for manga. Most hail from a handful of universities, with as many as
eight graduating from Waseda University. Their university degrees are commonly unrelated to
publishing or any type of creative arts, with only two graduating with a literature degree. Masahiko
Ibaraki, WJs eighth editor-in-chief, recalled that ‘nobody in the WJ department was otaku’, by which he
meant that no one had a genuine love for manga and anime culture (Nakano 2019). Ibaraki and the
four editors-in-chief that immediately preceded and followed him all had no interest in and had never
read manga before they were assigned to WJ. According to Ibaraki, the WJ work culture was militarylike, characterized by all-male masculine ethos and fierce competition among editors. WJ’s fifth editorin-chief, Nobuhiko Horie, confessed that his assignment to WJ was an ‘arranged marriage’, not a love
marriage (Oricon News 2018). It was only in the mid-1980s that the company began to make a gesture
of asking employees their wishes concerning job assignments (See also Inaba 2016).
Even the most successful editor-in-chief at WJ cannot hold the position for long; they are typically
rotated to a different job after an average of five years as editor-in-chief. As editor-in-chief, Nobuhiko
Horie shepherded WJ to its all-time peak circulation of 6.53 million copies. Yet his tenure lasted only
four years; after WJ he was moved to Men’s Non-no, a men’s fashion magazine, and then to Bart,
a current affairs magazine (Maki 2016). Shueisha employees at all ranks are regularly rotated, not only
between different publications but also to completely different departments. Despite WJ‘s success,
its editors’ compensation remains on par with peers in less profitable sectors (Mannavi 2022).
The job security at Shueisha is extraordinary. For example, one former Shueisha editor, now in her
mid-50s, mentioned that out of the 25 colleagues she joined the publisher with almost 30 years ago,
only two have left. Similarly, editors-in-chief at Shueisha tend to stay until retirement, except for one
who left to start an independent manga production company. A few other editors have made such
transitions, such as Yohei Sadoshima from Kodansha, who founded a creator’s agency, and more
recently, Iyoku Akiyo, a former WJ editor, who formed a rights management firm leveraging his ties
with Akira Toriyama, the creator of Dragon Ball (Weekly Bunshun Editors 2023). Nevertheless, the
disproportionate media attention lavished on them underscores the rarity of such career shifts, as
the vast majority of manga editors are reluctant to step out of major publishers, given the scale of
their works and sufficient financial rewards.
Shueisha’s lack of diversity, absence of specialization, and uniform pay structure are evident
across all its departments and levels. Remarkably, the last eight Shueisha CEOs, all men, began and
ended their careers within the company, starting in their early 20s and retiring in their 60s (Diamond
Online 2019). While Shueisha does not publicly disclose employee salaries, it’s reasonable to estimate
that the CEO’s salary doesn’t exceed 50 million yen (approximately $400,000). In contrast, consider
Roger Lynch, an American who assumed the CEO role at Condé Nast in 2019, estimated to earn
around $10 million annually, 25 times more. Lynch’s career path, common among Western media
executives (Havens and Lotz 2017, 155–7), encompasses a tenure at Morgan Stanley following an
MBA, and subsequent CEO positions at media-tech companies worldwide, including Pandora in the
Bay area. Such career flexibility is rare among Japanese media companies like Shueisha, which
consistently promote executives from within, highlighting its divergence from Western counterparts
in neoliberal capitalism.
This section explores the inner workings of Shueisha’s WJ editing team, elucidating how its
adherence to traditional Japanese human resources management starkly contrasts with the neolib­
eral working conditions characteristic of Western creative industries. The remarkable job security,
frequent job rotations, and stable working conditions at Shueisha offer insights into a distinct
organizational culture that diverges from the volatile nature of many Western creative sectors, as
documented in the literature. This foundational understanding sets the stage for an exploration of
the dynamics between WJ editors and freelance mangaka, as we transition to the next section.

Editors’ collaboration relationship with mangaka
How do editors as creative managers work with mangaka, and how is creativity pursued in
a situation where creativity and precarity seem to be decoupled? I will use several published
interviews with the current Jump+ editor Mr. Lin Shihei, and other renowned editors in the
past to describe editing processes and the key roles and responsibilities of editors at WJ in
order to understand the collaborative production system of manga (Lin 2020; 1101.com
2023).
The editors at WJ are continuously on the lookout for promising new manga artists. They actively
scout talent by holding awards for emerging mangaka, attending scouting events nationwide,
participating in conventions like Comiket, and through outreach on social media. The editor who
makes the initial contact, whether by receiving an in-person submission or fielding an unsolicited
inquiry before rival editors, secures exclusive rights to the mangaka. The relationship between
editors and mangaka lasts until an editor transfers out of WJ. This editor-mangaka bond persists
until the editor leaves WJ. However, mangaka don’t get to choose their editors, a dynamic sometimes
referred to as the ‘editor lottery,’ reflecting its unpredictable nature and the significant impact it can
have on their careers.
Currently, Lin stands out as perhaps the most renowned Shōnen Jump editor, having introduced
the world to mega-hits such as ‘Chainsaw Man’ by Tatsuki Fujimoto and ‘Spy x Family’ over his 17
years of experience. In one interview, he revealed to the surprise of the interviewers that he currently
oversees around 100 mangakas at various points in their careers, from novices to successful
experienced mangaka. Though only seven of them have serializations in Shōnen Jump, he commu­
nicates with the rest on a regular basis. At Shōnen Jump, nothing is valued more than guiding new
aspiring mangaka to serialization. Editors like Lin play a crucial role in guiding young mangakas,
helping them evolve their initial ideas into fully realized manga over a considerable period taking as
long as 10 years. This often starts with entries for various newcomer awards, leading to a one-shot
manga feature in Shōnen Jump or one of its affiliate publications. If a mangaka consistently produces
successful one-shots, the editor might then advocate at the serialization meetings for its serialization
in Shōnen Jump – a significant landmark that can herald a substantial financial turnaround for the
artist. Indeed, Lin volunteered to manage Fujimoto when he entered the award at the age of just 17.
Fujimoto had been sending Lin new works every month until his eventual debut seven years later on
Jump+, an online edition of Shōnen Jump.
As previously mentioned, most editors at WJ are college graduates without prior work experience
or specialized education. They undergo on-the-job training with minimal mentoring from more
experienced editors. These editors are often in competition with each other, not for higher pay but
for peer recognition and job security within WJ. Skilled editors like Lin have emphasized that their
learning process involves numerous trials and errors. For instance, in the year 2020, Lin brought more
than 55 one-off mangas to publication while handling at least five ongoing serializations for 52
weeks. In each of these over 300 instances, he closely observed and adapted to immediate reader
responses to refine his ‘sensitivity’, or industry lore, to discern what resonated with the audience.
While widely seen as a hitmaker, Lin acknowledges possessing no secret formula for creating a hit
manga: often, it is contingent on luck and the sheer volume of production, a luxury available to
editors with higher job security.
In regular meetings with mangaka lasting anywhere from one to five hours, in a café, Denny’s or
mangaka’s studio, editors offer guidance on plot development, character design, pacing, and other
storytelling aspects.
The collaboration between WJ editors and mangakas represents a delicate interplay of contrast­
ing yet complementary skills. A prime example is Kazuhiko Torishima, a former WJ editor-in-chief,
renowned for his analytical and methodological approach. His meticulous study of a 1970s manga by
Chiba Tetsuya, undertaken more than 50 times in the first months after his initial, albeit reluctant,
assignment to WJ, exemplifies this commitment (Keio Times 2017). Torishima’s in-depth analysis of

genre-specific elements, such as panel layouts and narrative angles, underpins his editorial success.
This analytical rigor was demonstrated, for example, in his mentorship of Toriyama Akira, who would
later create Dragon Ball, where he rejected over 500 pages of Toriyama’s early works. This under­
scores the high editorial standards and diverse dynamics within the mangaka-editor relationship.
However, the dynamics between editors and mangaka vary depending on experience and
personal preference. Editors also frequently suggest revisions to enhance the quality of manga.
Some mangaka explicitly asks editors not to give any creative advice (Sato 2012), while others
would appreciate detailed advice, as WJ editors are generally regarded as methodological and
analytical.
While editors typically hold degrees from prestigious universities, mangaka often come from
more modest backgrounds. For instance, among WJ’s top 10 best-selling mangaka, only one has
a college degree. They debut as professional mangaka for WJ at an average age of 21, with no formal
training in design, art, or any other creative discipline. Therefore, they tend to lack social skills, life
experience, or cultural capital. These gaps require editors to sometimes act in a supportive manner
but can also lead to patronizing and, at times, domineering behavior (e.g. Maki 2016, 205–14).6 Lin
emphasized how editors serve as mentors, overseeing the eating, sleep, and exercise routines of
mangaka. He even assisted a mangaka in searching for housing upon achieving success and signed
off the contract as a contract guarantor, a not uncommon practice among editors. Editors also
commonly kept mangaka informed about recent cultural trends, introducing them to books, other
manga, films, and cultural texts that were currently popular. Lin sends lots of films and books to the
mangaka to help them keep up with trends, nurture new ideas and sustain their creative sparks,
which is crucial given their busy schedules; this exposure also helps bridge any cultural gaps
stemming from the managakas diverse, often self-taught backgrounds.
In WJ, ‘Readers’ Poll’ are the ultimate indicators that determine the fate of a manga; they are the
supreme decree to which editors and manga artists pay the utmost attention, as if it were a divine
revelation (Myway Mook 2018; Nakano 2019, ; Nishimura 1994; Sunami 2014). The ‘Readers’ Poll” is
a mail-in survey where readers vote for their favorite manga series and characters. This poll, elevated
to a mythic level via repeated portrayals in popular culture, plays a pivotal role in deciding the fate of
both established and emerging mangaka and their respective series. The final decision rests with the
serialization meetings attended by the editor-in-chief and senior editors, but these decisions rarely
contradict the poll’s outcome. High-ranking manga series stand a strong chance of continued
serialization, benefiting from enhanced visibility and marketing support. In contrast, low-ranking
series risk cancellation to make room for new serialization. The phrase ‘serialization canceled,’
whether delivered via text, email, or in-person, is the most dreaded by mangaka (Maki 2016). Such
a decision can drastically affect their livelihood, whereas editors remain insulated due to their
lifetime employment status.

Uncreativity in action: editor-mangaka dynamics at WJ
The interaction between editors and mangaka at WJ illustrates the concept of ‘uncreativity’ in which
structured guidance, repetitive tasks, and stability are as crucial as creative sparks in bringing manga
visions to fruition in the production of manga.
This is particularly evident in the job security of editors as compared to the precarious freelance
nature of mangaka. Lin sarcastically introduced an industry saying: a seasoned WJ editor stands on
the corpses of tens or hundreds of mangaka (1101.com 2023). The relationship is indeed asymme­
trical and marked by a rigid hierarchy, which causes friction and tensions, as documented in many
accounts by mangaka (Aoki 2019) and editors (e.g. Kimura 2015; Sato 2012). At the same time, he and
other editors conscientiously strive to mitigate the unpredictable career trajectory of mangaka, and
help them to align their creative outputs with cultural trends and market preferences. With the
freedom and long-term thinking afforded by job security, editors can advocate for the development
of distinctive and long-lasting styles, innovative storytelling, and nurturing mangaka, for instance,

like Lin’s relationship with Fujimoto, to push the boundaries of the genre. This runs counter to the
increasingly common neoliberal narrative that associates freelancing with creative freedom, sug­
gesting instead that the assurance of stability can enhance creativity by allowing for long-term
planning and ambitious projects without the immediate pressure of financial success.
In manga production, the collaboration between mangakas and WJ editors is marked by a unique
interplay of creativity and methodology. Mangakas provide spontaneous creativity, while editors
often apply structured, analytical insights. This partnership could transcend professional interactions,
deeply influencing the mangaka’s work. The ‘Readers’ Poll’ exemplifies this dynamic, aligning
creative directions with audience preferences and balancing artistic vision with market feedback.
Through Bilton’s ‘uncreativity’ lens lens (2015), this relationship showcases a fusion of editorial preci­
sion and mangaka creativity, crucial for both the artistic integrity and commercial success of manga. The
‘Readers’ Poll’ emerges as a key indicator, validating the effectiveness of this collaborative process.

Conclusion: embracing the paradox of uncreative workers
In concluding our exploration into Shueisha’s world and the career trajectories of its editors, we
grapple with a striking paradox. It defies the prevalent Western notions of creative labor by revealing
how elite university graduates, endowed with broad cultural capital but often lacking training or
relevant work experience, join the manga industry through Shueisha. Their motivations are complex,
often driven by financial rewards, job stability, and societal status rather than a single-minded
passion for manga. This stands in contrast to the romanticized ideal of the impassioned creative
workers (Bhansing, Hitters, and Wijngaarden 2018), yet it is within this very framework that some
individuals, such as Shimatori and Lin, have risen to become era-defining editors who have sig­
nificantly impacted manga and Japanese popular culture more broadly.
This phenomenon calls into question the conventional wisdom that creative managers must be
fervently passionate and intrinsically motivated to succeed. Shueisha’s model demonstrates that
creative productivity and influential editorial work can also flourish within a structure prioritizing job
security, egalitarian principles, and a preference for generalist skills. This corporate context in
Shueisha and in some parts of Japanese creative industries more generally, fostering creativity in
a secure and organized environment, regardless of their original motivation, contrasts with the
Western discourse, which often correlates creativity with an environment that encourages auton­
omy, hyper-mobility, and self-enterprise.
The traditional trade-off at Shueisha, where employees sarcastically refer to themselves as ‘salary­
men,’ involves exchanging career autonomy for security by accepting frequent and random assign­
ments to different jobs. This might appear to be a surrender of creativity, where the freedom to
pursue individual creative interests or develop specialized skills is often secondary to the needs of
the company. Yet, such an interpretation may overlook an alternative manifestation of creativity.
Shueisha editors, including figures such as Lin and Shimatori, don’t use the term ‘creativity,’ but they
often express a drive to engage with ‘interesting work.’ For Lin, this even involved tasks like picking
up the manga draft on the last train from Tokyo to a mangaka’s studio in the suburbs (1101.com
2023). This distinction suggests that creativity is not limited to artistic pursuits but can also
encompass a wide spectrum of work activities. From this perspective, the seeming surrender of
control can be seen as a pathway to diversified engagement, fostering a dynamic interplay of skills
and roles. This aligns with a broader Japanese cultural appreciation, and sometimes a societal
expectation, to find satisfaction in work that is not narrowly defined by innovation but also includes
finding meaning and joy in varied responsibilities. Therefore, what might be misconstrued as an
abandonment of creativity may, in reality, be an alternative interpretation of creativity-one that
embraces the many ways individuals can find their work interesting and fulfilling.
The Shueisha narrative ultimately prompts a reassessment of the creative labor paradigm,
suggesting that a stable and structured environment can, in some circumstances, be conducive
to innovation. The company’s ethos fosters a culture where creativity is a collective pursuit,

benefiting from long-term stability and strategic corporate direction. At first glance, it appears
to be the conditions that enable ‘good work’ in cultural industries that normative and social
justice approach scholars have been exploring (Banks 2017; Hesmondhalgh and Baker 2011).
However, the Shueisha model is not without its critiques, as it does not fully address issues
such as the lack of autonomy deficits and rigid hierarchical structures, factors that have
propelled creative workers globally towards self-employment and freelancing in the first
place (Ross 2009, 5–6).
Nevertheless, the story of Shueisha’s WJ and its editors, an untold tale of achieving global
influence without aligning itself with the Western neoliberal model, beckons us to expand and
complicate our understanding of creative labor. Echoing Fung’s (2017, 202) challenge to the very
notion of ‘creativity,’ it underscores the impact of socio-political contexts on creative industries and
the laborers within them, resulting in diverse conceptions of creativity. This recognition is crucial as
the creative industries’ global landscape is evolving toward divergence as much as convergence
(Oyama and Lolli 2016), necessitating a more nuanced and contextualized understanding of the
diverse terrains of creative work that enables us to imagine alternative and improved futures for
creative works and industries.
