### Key Insights

**Manga Industry & Editorial Models:**
- Shueisha's lifetime employment model enables long-term mentorship and innovative franchises despite lack of editor specialization
- Creative managers (editors) enjoy security while primary creators (mangaka) face precarity—inverse of Western "creative class" rhetoric
- "Uncreativity" (methodology, rigor, stability) complements artist creativity, producing global cultural exports

**Music & Postmodern Culture:**
- YMO exemplified postmodern Japan (1978–1985): political-to-cultural shift; hybridity, pastiche, irony
- Jazz-fusion training distinguished YMO from Western DIY synth-pop; professional studio musicianship created polished mass-appeal sound
- YMO's self-orientalisation strategy: knowingly deployed techno-Orientalist imagery for market access while maintaining ironic distance
- Video game music connection: YMO legitimized arcade culture as artistic form; anticipated digital/"otaku" aesthetics

**Soft Power & Regional Circulation:**
- Japanese manga, music, fashion, design circulating regionally and globally as soft power
- YMO framed as symbol of Japan's technological dominance; influenced electro, hip-hop, ambient, techno globally
- Postmodern Japan aesthetics (hybridity, minimalism, futurism) exported through fashion, design, music
- Academic gap: Despite $21–24B franchises and global cultural influence, Japanese creative industries understudied relative to importance

**Theory & De-Westernization:**
- Regional circulation, soft power mechanisms, creative labor models diverge from Western frameworks
- De-Westernization scholarship emerging: challenge to assumption that theory/innovation flows unidirectionally from West
- YMO and postmodern Japan offer alternative, de-colonial perspectives on modernity, technology, creativity
- East Asian scholars increasingly contextualize own creative industries rather than importing Western theory wholesale
