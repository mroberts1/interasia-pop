## Overview

A manga editor at a major Japanese publisher like [[Shueisha]] functions as a **creative manager** — an intermediary between profit-focused executives and primary creative workers (mangaka). At [[Weekly Shōnen Jump]], editors are central to both the artistic development of manga and the commercial success of the publication.

## Scholarly Classification

Following Hesmondhalgh & Baker (2011), manga editors are classified as **creative managers**, distinct from:
- **Primary creative workers:** Mangaka (authors/illustrators), who create original content
- **Administrators:** Support staff
- **Executives:** Management/ownership
- **Unskilled labor:** Production workers

## Hiring & Background

### Education & Experience

- Typically **elite university graduates** hired through [[Shukatsu]]
- Often **lack specialization** in publishing, media, or creative arts
- Rarely possess **prior work experience** in manga or media industries
- Many **never read manga** before assignment to [[Weekly Shōnen Jump]]

### Career Entry

- Entry as creative manager at age 22–25
- Assigned to manga division after general company training
- Begin career with minimal mentoring from experienced editors
- Learn through on-the-job trial-and-error and observation of reader response

### Examples

- **Masahiko Ibaraki** (8th editor-in-chief): "Nobody in the WJ department was otaku" (lacked genuine manga passion)
- **Nobuhiko Horie** (5th editor-in-chief): Described his assignment to WJ as an "arranged marriage, not a love marriage"
- **Editors historically:** 8 of 11 WJ editors-in-chief graduated from Waseda University; most with non-literature degrees

## Responsibilities & Workflow

### Talent Scouting

Editors actively recruit new mangaka through:
- Manga awards for emerging artists
- Nationwide scouting events and conventions (e.g., Comiket)
- Social media outreach
- Unsolicited submissions

**Editor Advantage:** First editor to make contact with a promising artist secures exclusive rights; creates competitive dynamic among editors.

### Artist Development

Editors guide mangaka through multiple stages:
1. **Entry Awards:** Submission to newcomer competitions
2. **One-Shot Publication:** Stories published in [[Weekly Shōnen Jump]] or affiliate publications
3. **Pre-Serialization Development:** Repeated submissions and feedback cycles (can last 10+ years)
4. **Serialization Advocacy:** Pitching at serialization meetings to secure ongoing series slot

**Example:** Lin Shihei's 7-year mentorship of Tatsuki Fujimoto (Chainsaw Man), from initial age-17 submission to Jump+ serialization debut.

### Editorial Guidance

In regular meetings (1–5 hours, often in cafés or mangaka studios), editors advise on:
- Plot development and pacing
- Character design and development
- Narrative structure and panel layout
- Visual storytelling techniques
- Alignment with reader preferences and market trends

### Mentorship & Support

Editors often extend beyond editorial roles to provide:
- **Personal mentorship:** Monitoring sleep, exercise, nutrition
- **Cultural enrichment:** Introducing books, films, and cultural trends to keep artists current
- **Practical support:** Helping with housing searches, contract guarantor roles
- **Emotional support:** Managing career volatility and cancellation risks

### Reader Response Management

Editors monitor and interpret:
- **Readers' Polls:** Weekly mail-in voting on favorite series and characters
- **Circulation data:** Sales and engagement metrics
- **Fan feedback:** Social media and community responses
- **Series performance:** Positioning of manga within the magazine

## Collaboration Model: "Uncreativity"

### Editorial Methodology

Drawing on Chris Bilton's concept, manga editors embody **"uncreativity"** — structured guidance balanced against artist creativity:

- **Analytical Rigor:** Deep study of genre conventions, narrative techniques, panel layouts (e.g., Kazuhiko Torishima's repeated analysis of manga by Chiba Tetsuya)
- **Critical Feedback:** Rejection of weak ideas (e.g., Torishima's rejection of 500+ pages of Toriyama's early Dragon Ball work)
- **Methodical Development:** Step-by-step guidance preventing premature failure or commercial disaster
- **Reader Integration:** Using Readers' Polls as data-driven creative input

### Power Dynamics

The editor-mangaka relationship is **asymmetrical**:
- **Editors:** Permanent employment, job security, institutional authority
- **Mangaka:** Freelance status, precarious income, dependent on editor advocacy
- **Industry saying:** "A seasoned [[Weekly Shōnen Jump]] editor stands on the corpses of tens or hundreds of mangaka"

This hierarchy creates **friction and tension** (documented in mangaka memoirs and editor accounts) but also allows editors with **security and long-term thinking** to advocate for distinctive, innovative works.

## Workload & Skills

### Daily Reality

- **Volume-intensive work:** Managing multiple serializations simultaneously
- **Example (Lin Shihei, 2020):** 55+ one-shot submissions reviewed while handling 5+ ongoing serializations (52 weeks × 5 series = 260+ editorial sessions annually)
- **Speed of learning:** Developing "sensitivity" (industry lore) through rapid iteration and adaptation to immediate reader response

### Competitive Environment

- Editors compete with peers for **recognition and job security**, not salary increases
- Success measured by hit series and artist development records
- No secret formula acknowledged; success attributed to **luck and volume** (high-security environment allowing multiple attempts)

## Professional Identity & Motivation

### "Interesting Work" vs. "Creativity"

- Contemporary editors rarely use the term "creativity"
- Instead emphasize drive to engage in **"interesting work"** across diverse projects
- Example: Lin Shihei's commitment includes picking up manga drafts from distant studios via late-night trains

### Salary & Compensation

- Young editors (20s): Up to 10 million yen annually (~$93,000)
- Significantly above 95% of Japanese workforce
- **Paradox:** Despite WJ's global success and profitability, editor compensation remains **on par with peers in less profitable sectors**
- No performance-based bonuses or commission structures

## Career Progression

### Rotation & Mobility

- Employees regularly rotated between divisions
- **Even successful editors:** Moved to different magazines after term (e.g., Horie to Men's Non-no, then Bart after peak WJ success)
- **Typical editor-in-chief tenure:** 5 years before rotation to different department

### Job Security

- Exceptional retention rates: One former editor noted 23 of 25 colleagues from her hiring cohort 30 years prior remained at Shueisha
- Editors-in-chief remain until retirement (except rare exceptions founding independent companies)
- Few leave to start independent ventures; those who do become rare exceptions (e.g., Yohei Sadoshima founding creator agency)

## Gender Representation

- **All 11 [[Weekly Shōnen Jump]] editors-in-chief:** Men
- **Lack of diversity:** Concentrated among elite university graduates
- **Gender imbalance:** Particularly pronounced in senior editorial roles

## Innovations & Influence

### Editorial Lore

Editors' presumptions about commercial viability — "industry lore" — significantly shape:
- Range of manga perceived as commercially successful
- Editorial decisions on serialization and cancellation
- Broader trends in manga content and style

### Cultural Impact

Through mentorship and editorial vision, editors shape:
- Globally influential franchises (Dragon Ball, One Piece, Chainsaw Man, Spy x Family)
- Evolution of manga aesthetics and storytelling techniques
- Circulation of Japanese popular culture across Asia and globally

## Challenges & Critiques

### Structural Issues

1. **Rigid hierarchy:** Limits autonomy and creative choice
2. **Power imbalance:** Secure editors, precarious artists
3. **Lack of diversity:** Elite-university concentration
4. **Lack of specialization:** Generalist hiring may miss passionate candidates
5. **Exploitation risk:** Asymmetrical relationship can enable domineering behavior

## Comparison: Western Creative Managers

| Aspect | Japanese Manga Editor (Shueisha) | Western Creative Manager |
|---|---|---|
| **Hiring** | Elite graduates, no specialization | Experienced professionals, relevant background |
| **Employment** | Lifetime contract | At-will, fluid mobility |
| **Job Security** | Exceptional | Precarious; subject to market demands |
| **Compensation** | Fixed salary by rank/tenure | Performance-based, variable |
| **Career Path** | Internal rotation | External mobility expected |
| **Artist Relationship** | Long-term, hierarchical mentorship | Project-based, more collaborative |
| **Creative Approach** | Methodical; "uncreativity" valued | Autonomous; artist-led creativity emphasized |

## Connection to Inter-Asia Pop

Manga editors at [[Shueisha]] are central figures in:
- Development of manga franchises exported across Asia
- Regional circulation and adaptation of Japanese media
- Transnational fandom and audience engagement
- Soft power and cultural influence of Japan in Asia and globally
