# Shibuya-kei

**Shibuya-kei** (渋谷系) is a style of independent popular music that emerged in Japan in the late 1980s, characterized by ostentatious internationalism, postmodern pastiche, and eclectic appropriation of retro genres.

## Origins and Definition

The term "Shibuya-kei" (literally "Shibuya style") emerged in the early 1990s to describe a loose-knit network of [[Japanese indie musicians|Indie Music Scenes]], DJs, and independent labels centered in the fashionable Shibuya shopping district of south-west Tokyo. Unlike mainstream Japanese pop distributed by major labels, Shibuya-kei was defined by its aesthetic stance against the mainstream.

The movement emerged from a rich culture of music collecting and consumption enabled by the opening of flagship record stores—HMV, Tower Records, Wave, and Shinseido—which provided outlets for both Japanese and imported foreign music. Two key figures regarded as most influential in the movement's emergence were **Konishi Yasuharu** (founder of [[pizzicato-five]]) and **Oyamada Keigo** (founder of Flipper's Guitar and later known as [[Cornelius]]), both avid record collectors.

## Musical Characteristics

Shibuya-kei is defined by:

- **Eclectic internationalism**: Fusing [[jazz]], [[easy listening]], [[bossa nova]], British and American retro-pop, 1960s French pop, [[exotica]], European film soundtracks, Motown, disco, and 1980s UK indie-pop
- **Postmodern pastiche and sampling**: Drawing on pre-existing media; reintroduction of obscure or retro recordings to contemporary audiences
- **Retro-futurism**: Particular fascination with 1950s-1960s [[techno-futurism]], "space-age bachelor pad" aesthetics, and [[saudade do futuro|"nostalgia for the future"]]
- **Emotional ambivalence**: Happy-sad tonality; joy tinged with nostalgia, loss, and wistfulness ([[saudade]], [[mono no aware]])
- **Lifestyle aesthetics**: Lyrical focus on leisure, consumption, nightlife, fashion, cinema, café culture rather than social critique or angst

## Key Figures and Bands

- [[pizzicato-five]]: Fronted by the glamorous [[pizzicato-five]]; iconic postmodern mélange of jazz, easy listening, Motown, disco
- [[Flipper's Guitar]]: Indebted to British indie-pop and neo-psychedelia
- [[Cornelius]] (Oyamada Keigo): Later solo project; continuation of Shibuya-kei internationalism
- [[Kahimi Karie]]: Shibuya-kei vocalist; lived in Paris; influenced by 1960s chanteuses
- [[Takako Minegawa]], [[Yukari Fresh]], [[Bridge]], Love Tambourines: Other notable artists

## Record Labels

The movement was anchored by independent Japanese labels:

- [[Readymade]]: Founded by Konishi Yasuharu
- [[Trattoria]]: Founded by Oyamada Keigo; included Western artists
- [[Crue-L]]: Released Kahimi Karie's _Mike Alway's Diary_ (1992)
- [[Escalator]]: Distribution platform for Shibuya-kei artists

## Transnational Connections

[[Martin Roberts]] emphasizes Shibuya-kei as a **[[transnational soundscape]]**—not merely a Japanese phenomenon but "an ongoing international co-production involving both Japanese and non-Japanese musics and musicians."

### London-Paris Circuit
The movement maintained close ties to British art-pop via [[él Records]] (Mike Alway, Philippe Auclair/Louis Philippe) and to French retro-pop aesthetics. Kahimi Karie's _Mike Alway's Diary_ was an homage to él Records founder; él Records artists such as The Monochrome Set were featured on Oyamada's Trattoria label compilation _Fab Gear_ (1991).

### US Distribution
Outside Japan, Shibuya-kei was distributed across European and US indie labels: Paris (Tricatel), London (Cherry Red), Berlin (Bungalow), New York (Matador), Chicago (Minty Fresh), LA (Emperor Norton). [[Matador Records]] played a key role in popularizing [[pizzicato-five]] for American audiences.

### New York Scene
- [[pizzicato-five]] performed at New York New Music Seminar (1992-1994)
- [[Cornelius]] debuted _Fantasma_ (1998) at South-by-Southwest
- [[Towa Tei]] (Japanese-Korean DJ, formerly of Deee-Lite) promoted Shibuya-kei in NYC acid-jazz scene; collaborated with [[pizzicato-five]]
- [[Cibo Matto]]: Japanese expatriate group often associated with Shibuya-kei

### Brazil Connection
Bossa nova holds a special place in Shibuya-kei due to Japan-Brazil historical migration links and shared aesthetic of romantic nostalgia ([[saudade]]). Contemporary collaboration: [[pizzicato-five]] and Fernanda Takai (Pato Fu) released _Maki-Takai: No Jetlag_ (2009), performing in both Japanese and Portuguese.

## Historical Timeline

- **Late 1980s**: Record store ecosystem in Shibuya; music collector culture flourishes
- **Early 1990s**: Movement peak; Pizzicato Five debut (1992); Flipper's Guitar emergence; independent label activity
- **Mid-1990s**: Western indie label interest; international tours; Western musician involvement (Mike Alway, Philippe Auclair, Nick Currie/Momus)
- **Late 1990s**: Web/internet fan communities proliferate; Momus's ironic 1998 "Shibuya-kei is dead" declaration
- **Early 2000s**: Mainstream J-pop absorption; vanguardist dismissals of genre's peak
- **Post-2000s**: [[Neo-Shibuya-kei]] global movements; continued collaborations; archival/tribute culture
- **2011**: Pizzicato One release (post-Tōhoku earthquake); [[mono no aware]] becomes national metaphor

## Global Influence

By the 2000s-2010s, Shibuya-kei inspired artists globally:

- **UK**: Gentle People; The Pearlfishers
- **France**: Dimitri From Paris; Nouvelle Vague
- **US**: The Flaming Lips; The Bird and The Bee
- **Spain**: La Casa Azul
- **Argentina**: Modular
- **South Korea**: Clazziquai Project; Casker; Humming Urban Stereo (neo-Shibuya-kei movement)

The Shibuya-kei Yahoo! Group lists dozens of "like-minded acts" from France, US, UK, Spain, Sweden, Netherlands.

## Affect and Aesthetic Philosophy

Shibuya-kei is characterized by emotional and aesthetic ambivalence:

- **Happy-Sad tonality**: Happiness juxtaposed with loss, nostalgia, wistfulness. _Happy Sad_ (EP), _Happy End of the World_ (album) exemplify this.
- **[[Saudade]]**: Portuguese concept of profound bittersweet nostalgia; parallel to Shibuya-kei mood
- **[[Mono no aware]]**: Japanese concept of "pathos of things"; transience and gentle sadness. Evoked in [[mono no aware|Momus's "The Sadness of Things"]] (1995)
- **[[Saudade do futuro]]**: "Nostalgia for the future"; yearning for 1960s techno-utopian promise (space travel, modern design, stereophonic technology) repackaged as postmodern irony
- **Commodity culture utopia**: Consumption of culturally refined "right" items as path to better living; leisure and play over work

## Context: [[Retromania]] and Postmodernism

Shibuya-kei's retromania—relentless nostalgia for 1950s-1960s popular music—is not uniquely Japanese but symptomatic of larger postmodern aesthetic shifts in Western popular culture. However, what differentiated Shibuya-kei was Japanese artists' distinctive "mark" on retro-pop genres, creating an autonomous hybridized soundscape in its own right.

[[Simon Reynolds]] notes this in _Retromania_ (2011): Shibuya-kei anticipated Western postmodern retromania trends and is often depicted in Western discourse as "turning Japanese"—yet Roberts argues the reverse: J-pop retromania is symptomatic of postmodernity at large, not a uniquely Japanese phenomenon.

## Decline and Persistence

By 2004, Western critical consensus declared Shibuya-kei "dead" (David Marx's periodization; Nick Currie's 1998 pronouncement). However, the style has "stubbornly refused to go away." Revivals include:

- Flipper's Guitar tributes
- Shibuya-kei remixes and retrospectives
- Neo-Shibuya-kei bands (Qypthone, Capsule, Dahlia, Elektel, Aprils)
- Japanese Shibuya-kei artists remain actively engaged in collaborations with non-Japanese musicians globally
