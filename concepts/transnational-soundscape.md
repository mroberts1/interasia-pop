# Transnational Soundscape

**Transnational Soundscape** is a theoretical framework developed by musicologist [[Martin Roberts]] to understand [[Shibuya-kei]] and other contemporary music forms as **ongoing international co-productions involving both local and non-local musics and musicians**, rather than as phenomena bound by national geography or ethnic identity.

## Theoretical Framework

Roberts proposes Shibuya-kei be understood not primarily as a "Japanese" genre but as a deterritorialized, globally-circulating musical and aesthetic phenomenon. Key aspects:

### De-territorialization
Rather than anchoring Shibuya-kei to Japan or treating it as exotic Other from Western perspective, Roberts "dis-locates" it from the local frame of reference and "deterritorializes" it. The movement originated in Japan but circulated via international networks from inception.

### International Co-production
Shibuya-kei was from its beginning "predicated on its eclectic appropriation and transculturation of a variety of Western and other popular musics which were already in transnational circulation." It involved:

- **Japanese musicians** engaging Western popular music traditions (1960s French/British pop, bossa nova, easy listening)
- **Western musicians and labels** promoting and remixing Shibuya-kei material internationally
- **Expatriate networks**: Japanese musicians living in Paris, New York; Western musicians resident in Japan; Japanese-Korean and Japanese-Brazilian artists
- **Physical and virtual circulation**: International touring, remix releases, website fan communities, web-based distribution

### Audiotopia
Roberts invokes Josh Kun's concept of [[audiotopia]]—music that is "simultaneously everywhere and nowhere in particular." Shibuya-kei has emerged as an "autonomous, hybridised soundscape in its own right" that constitutes an audiotopia.

## London-Paris-Tokyo-New York-Rio Circuit

Rather than isolated geographic origins, Shibuya-kei is understood as a product of international musical networks:

### London-Paris
- [[él Records]] (British art-pop label, 1980s) inspired [[Crue-L Records]] in Japan
- [[Philippe Auclair]] (Louis Philippe) and [[Nick Currie]] (Momus) maintained careers across UK/France/Japan
- [[Kahimi Karie]] lived in Paris; styled herself after 1960s French chanteuses
- Cross-Atlantic indie label circulation: Cherry Red (London), Tricatel (Paris) distributed Shibuya-kei material

### New York Circuit
- [[Pizzicato Five]] and [[Cornelius]] performed at US festivals and music venues (1992-1998)
- [[Matador Records]] (New York) distributed Pizzicato Five; sales reportedly over 100,000 copies
- [[Towa Tei]] (Japanese-Korean DJ) bridged Deee-Lite acid-jazz scene with Shibuya-kei
- NYC venue performances, remix culture, club DJ circulation

### Rio-Tokyo Connection
- **Japan-Brazil migration history**: Historical connection through Japanese diaspora in Brazil
- **Bossa nova adoption**: [[Saudade]] parallels to Shibuya-kei happy-sad tonality
- **Contemporary collaborations**: [[Nomiya Maki]] (Pizzicato Five) and [[Fernanda Takai]] (Pato Fu, Japanese-Brazilian) performed together; released _Maki-Takai: No Jetlag_ (2009) with bilingual Japanese-Portuguese vocals

### Berlin and Distributed Networks
- [[Bungalow Records]] (Berlin) released _Sushi_ compilations; circulated Shibuya-kei widely across Europe/US
- Multiple European and North American indie labels participated in distribution: Matador (NY), Minty Fresh (Chicago), Emperor Norton (LA), Readymade International (distributed via subsidiaries)

## Global Shibuya-kei: Neo-movements and Influence

The transnational soundscape framework explains Shibuya-kei's continuing evolution and global influence:

### European Artists
- **France**: Dimitri From Paris (DJ), Nouvelle Vague (indie band)
- **UK**: Gentle People, The Pearlfishers
- **Spain**: La Casa Azul (neo-Shibuya-kei album _Cerca de Shibuya_ 2011)

### American/North American
- **US**: The Flaming Lips, The Bird and The Bee, Apples in Stereo
- **Canada**: Influenced artists

### East/Southeast Asian
- **South Korea**: Clazziquai Project, Casker, Humming Urban Stereo (neo-Shibuya-kei movement)

### Latin American
- **Argentina**: Modular (neo-Shibuya-kei albums)
- **Brazil**: Contemporary collaborations

### Web-Based Distribution
- Shibuya-kei Yahoo! Group (launched 2000): Lists dozens of "like-minded acts" from France, US, UK, Spain, Sweden, Netherlands
- Fan-driven internationalization via blogs, forums, social networks (late 1990s-2010s)
- YouTube, streaming services, torrent sites: Music readily available globally by 2000s-2010s

## Relationship to Postmodernism

The transnational soundscape is inseparable from postmodern culture:

- **Horizontal vs. vertical influence**: Rather than direct "vertical" reference to Euro-American retro genres, Shibuya-kei responds to "horizontal" parallel shifts in postmodern music culture globally (retromania, pastiche, sampling, camp nostalgia)
- **Postmodernity at large**: Shibuya-kei's retromania is symptomatic of Western postmodernity generally, not uniquely Japanese—challenging orientalist frame of Japan as "empire of retro"
- **Eclectic citation**: Pastiche, sampling, ironic historicism, appropriation of pre-existing media—all postmodern operations—are central to Shibuya-kei's method

## Comparison to Other Transnational Music Forms

Roberts notes parallel frameworks:

- **Diaspora-based music** (South Asian bhangra, Algerian raï): Often tied to ethnic/national communities displaced from homeland
- **Shibuya-kei as different**: Producers not exclusively from one ethnic group or national community; includes Japanese, Japanese-Korean, Japanese-Brazilian, Western non-Japanese participants; not primarily diaspora-inflected but cosmopolitan/international by design

## Affect: [[Saudade]], [[Mono no Aware]], [[Saudade do Futuro]]

The transnational soundscape produces a distinctive affect:

- **[[Saudade]]** (Portuguese): Profound, bittersweet nostalgia; central to Brazilian bossa nova; parallel to Shibuya-kei emotional register
- **[[Mono no aware]]** (Japanese): "Pathos of things"; transience and gentle sadness; aesthetic awareness of impermanence
- **[[Saudade do futuro]]**: "Nostalgia for the future"; yearning for 1960s technological utopian promise (space travel, modern design, stereophonic hi-fi) now experienced through postmodern irony
- **Happy-sad ambivalence**: Simultaneous joy and melancholy; pleasure and loss

These affects transcend national categories and circulate transnationally across the soundscape.

## Critical Implications

Understanding Shibuya-kei as transnational soundscape challenges:

- **Nationalist music historiography**: Questioning "national" categories as primary frame for popular music analysis
- **Western exoticization**: Resisting frame of Japan/J-pop as exotic Other; instead emphasizing collaborative international production
- **Ethnic/diaspora assumptions**: Showing cosmopolitan internationalism (not diaspora-based) as organizing principle

Roberts argues this framework requires "us to rethink the place of the national in relation to popular music" more broadly.

## Continued Evolution

Japanese Shibuya-kei artists remain actively involved in collaborative projects with non-Japanese musicians globally, ensuring "the style continues to follow new and intriguing itineraries in the present." The transnational soundscape is not historical artifact but ongoing process.
